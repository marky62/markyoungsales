# MYS YouTube Scripting Skill

YouTube scripts for Mark Young Sales have one job: take a field salesperson
who clicked because the title named their problem, and walk them through a
structured argument that makes them feel understood, makes them see something
differently, and leaves them wanting more. The sale happens later. The video's
job is trust and insight.

---

## Pre-Writing Checklist

Do not start writing until all of the following are confirmed:

- [ ] Title is locked (run mys-title-thumbnail skill if not)
- [ ] Thumbnail text is locked
- [ ] The single core idea is defined — one specific thing the viewer will
      think differently about by the end
- [ ] The viewer is the **Sales Manager running a team of area managers**
      (the default audience). If this script addresses an area manager
      instead, there's a stated reason for the exception.
- [ ] What does that manager currently believe? What will the video challenge?
- [ ] Any worked examples needed have been identified — real territory
      situations from Mark's own experience, not Sarah Mitchell

---

## Writing Rules (Apply to Every Section)

These rules are non-negotiable and must be checked before presenting any draft.

**Full sentences at all times.** No fragments used as stylistic devices.
Each sentence does one job.

**Contractions throughout.** "Can't" not "cannot". "Don't" not "do not".
"It's" not "it is". "Haven't" not "have not". "Aren't" not "are not".
"Won't" not "will not". "Didn't" not "did not". The only exception is when
the emphasis specifically requires the full form.

**Never say "rep".** It's lazy and it's a little disrespectful about the people
the audience manages. Use **area manager** when referring to a specific member
of the team, **salesperson** or **field salesperson** when the reference is
general, or **your people** when speaking to the manager about the team as a
whole. Vary them so no one word gets worn out. This applies to every spoken
word and every piece of written copy.

The one exception is packaging built for search — a title or description may
carry "sales rep" where that is genuinely the phrase people type. Voice rules
govern what Mark says; search terms govern what gets found. Flag it when the
two collide rather than deciding silently.

**Conversational but authoritative.** This is a Head of Sales speaking from
experience, not a YouTuber performing for the camera. No hype. No exclamation
points used for energy. No rhetorical filler ("Now, here's the thing...").

**No borrowed statistics.** Never use the circulated marketing figures —
"7% of communication is words" (a misreading of Mehrabian, whose study was
about incongruent emotional signals, not communication in general), "it takes
seven hours to build trust", "eleven touchpoints before a sale". They're soft
at best and wrong at worst, and a channel whose entire position is evidence-led
precision can't afford to repeat them. If a number goes in a script, it's one
Mark can source or has lived.

**Teach the method. Keep the room.** Free content gives the methodology away.
The frameworks, the named systems, the reasoning, the numbers, the full logic
of how it works — all of it is fair game, in as much detail as the video can
carry. Withholding makes videos vague, and vague videos don't build authority.

What stays paid isn't information. It's the templates themselves, and Mark
working through one specific person's territory with them inside the cohort.
A viewer can learn exactly how the method works from YouTube and still have
every reason to join, because knowing the method and applying it to your own
170 accounts with someone watching are different things.

**The test:** would this video still be worth the viewer's time if they never
bought anything? If the answer is no because you held something back, put it
back in.

## Setup/Payoff Thread (Runs Through the Entire Script)

This is the most important structural principle and must be tracked
consciously throughout every script, not just in the hook.

**The principle:** Every statement that raises a question in the viewer's
mind is a setup. The payoff must come before they lose patience. If you set
something up and don't pay it off, viewers feel cheated and leave. If you
pay off too early (give the answer before the viewer wants it), they feel
nothing and leave.

**The viewer's curiosity should look like a wave, not a flatline.**

- The hook contains the macro setup for the whole video
- Each brick opens with a micro setup (the transition) that raises curiosity
- Each brick closes with a payoff that releases the tension
- The bridge is the final payoff for the macro setup from the hook
- The CTA comes after the final payoff — not before

**Track this actively:** Before finishing a draft, list every setup that
has been created and confirm each one has been paid off before the CTA.
If anything is set up and not paid off, it must be resolved or removed.

## Section 1 — Hook (First 30–60 seconds)

The hook is the highest-stakes section of the script. Its only job is to
pay off the click and earn the next 30 seconds. It doesn't introduce Mark.
It doesn't explain what the video will cover. It grabs.

### Hook Structure

**Line 1 — Mirror the title.**
The very first line should feel like the natural next sentence after the
title. If the title is "Your Pipeline Is Lying to You", the opening line
should land in that same space. "Your pipeline is lying to you" or
"Most of what's in your pipeline right now isn't going to close" — the
viewer clicked because the title named something they felt. Line 1
confirms they were right to click.

**Lines 2–4 — Name the problem specifically.**
Not the general topic — the specific feeling. What does this problem
feel like from the inside? What does the viewer do every week that makes
this problem real? Make them recognise their own situation, not a general
description of a problem category.

**Lines 5–8 — Set up the macro payoff.**
Tell the viewer what they'll have by the end of the video — but without
paying it off yet. Create the question, don't answer it. "By the end of
this, you'll know exactly why that's happening — and what to do about it"
is a setup. "Here's why that's happening: X" is a payoff. The setup
belongs in the hook. The payoff belongs in the body or bridge.

**The hook must not:**
- Introduce Mark by name and credentials before earning the right
- Explain the structure of the video ("In this video, I'm going to...")
- Ask a question the viewer might answer "no" to
- Use hype language that doesn't match the calm MYS delivery style
- Give away the answer before it's been earned

### Hook Length

Target: 150–250 words. Any shorter and it hasn't done the job. Any longer
and the viewer has already left.

## Section 2 — Body (The Bricks)

The body is built from bricks. Each brick makes one point. Together, the
bricks build the argument.

### Brick Structure

Each brick has three parts:

**Transition (Setup):** The sentence that opens the brick. It tells the
viewer what's coming in a way that makes them curious — without paying
anything off. This is the setup for this brick's payoff.

Example of a weak transition: "The next thing I want to talk about is
call management."
Example of a strong transition: "Here's where most of the wasted time
actually lives — and it's not where you'd expect."

**Transitions must stand completely alone.** Brick headings, and the
emotion/logic labels, are scaffolding for whoever is writing the script.
None of it is ever spoken. So a transition that only makes sense because
there's a heading sitting above it will land as a non-sequitur on camera,
and the viewer will feel the join.

**The test:** cover the heading and read the transition cold. Does it tell
the viewer what's coming and give them a reason to stay for it? Watch for
these three failure patterns in particular:

- **Pointing at the heading.** "Once you've got that, it's straightforward"
  — got what? The transition is relying on a title the viewer can't see.
- **Announcing rather than opening.** "Which brings us back to those two
  people from the start" tells the viewer where they are but gives them no
  reason to care. A transition that only navigates isn't a setup.
- **Naming the concept in the heading but not out loud.** If the brick is
  built on a named concept, the concept has to be introduced in the spoken
  words, not assumed from the label.

Every transition should survive being read on its own to somebody who
hasn't seen the script.

**Emotion half (Problem + Example):**
The problem is what the viewer currently experiences or believes.
The example makes it visible — a story, a scenario, or a concrete
illustration that lets them picture it.

**Sarah Mitchell is for the paid room, not for YouTube.** She's a
calculation vehicle — she exists to make arithmetic followable for someone
who has already bought. On the channel she's a weakness, because a fictional
area manager can't carry a scar and viewers can feel the difference between
an invented example and something that actually happened. Use real territory
situations from Mark's thirty years: the team, the account, the year it went
wrong. Anonymise as needed. Keep Sarah for the cohort.

The emotion half speaks to the fast brain. It should feel easy to follow,
not effortful. If it requires concentration to understand, simplify it.

**Logic half (Lesson/Payoff):**
The logical conclusion or reframe. This is the payoff for the brick's
setup. By the end of this half, the viewer should be able to say "I get
it." It doesn't need to be long — sometimes a single clear sentence is
the payoff. Don't over-explain once the point has landed.

### Brick Calibration

Not every point needs a full emotion half. If the point is simple and
immediately obvious, a long example wastes the viewer's time. Ask: does
this point need to be shown before it can be understood, or can it just
be stated and accepted?

If the point is counterintuitive, needs to feel real before the viewer
will believe it, or requires emotional buy-in — the emotion half needs
to be substantial. If the point is logical and self-evident once stated —
keep the emotion half short or skip it.

### Body Length

MYS videos should be substantive enough to feel worth the click, but not
padded. Target 3–5 bricks for most videos. A 5-brick video at proper
depth will run 6–9 minutes. Do not pad to hit an arbitrary length target.

### What the Body Must Not Do

- Hold back a step to protect the paid offer — teach the method properly
- Use jargon without explanation (CRM, COS, KPIs — fine if explained,
  not fine if assumed)
- Repeat the same point in different words across multiple bricks
- Overload with information — one point per brick, one brick at a time

## Section 3 — Bridge (30–60 seconds)

The bridge is the payoff for the macro setup created in the hook. It lands
the emotional conclusion before asking for anything.

**Its job:** Make the viewer feel the weight of what they've just learned,
and connect it to something bigger than the specific points made in the body.
This is where the "that's what this is really about" moment lives.

**MYS bridge pattern:** The core thesis of the channel — that most sales
problems are territory and account problems, not selling problems — is the
landing point for almost every video. The bridge is where that lands most
cleanly. Not stated didactically ("So what I want you to take from this is...")
but arrived at naturally ("Most people in this situation try to solve it by
calling more often or preparing better. That doesn't fix it, because that's
not where the problem is.")

The bridge must not:
- Introduce new information the viewer hasn't been prepared for
- Start the CTA before the conclusion has landed
- Be skipped — it's the connective tissue between argument and ask

## Section 4 — The Ending

Every video ends with exactly one ask. What that ask is depends on where the
video sits in the rotation.

### Never actually end the video

Don't wrap up. A recap, a "thanks for watching", or even an "OK, so that's
it" tells the viewer the session is over and hands them back to the
algorithm, which will happily send them to somebody else. Trust is banked in
runs of three and four videos, not in single ones.

Instead, hand off. Name the next problem the viewer now has, attach a cost to
leaving it, and point at the specific video that solves it:

> "So you know which accounts are carrying the territory. The next question
> is what you actually do with the ones that aren't — and getting that wrong
> is where most people lose a year. That's this video here."

Every piece of that is doing a job: it names the next problem so they realise
they have it, it makes solving it feel urgent, and it makes the next step
effortless. The video doesn't end, it continues inside the next one.

### Split the handoff: spoken names the problem, end screen names the video

Making videos one at a time means the ideal next video often doesn't exist
yet, so the handoff ends up pointing backwards into the catalogue. Two things
fix that.

**First, remember who's watching.** A viewer arriving at video 12 hasn't seen
videos 1 to 11. Pointing them at an older video isn't backwards to them, it's
forwards — the catalogue is a shelf, not a series, and chronology only exists
in Mark's head. Point at whichever video best serves the problem the viewer
now has, whenever it was made.

**Second, keep the spoken line and the pointer separate.** Name the *problem*
out loud; let the end screen name the *video*. End screens can be changed at
any time without re-filming or re-uploading, so when a better target is
published later, swap the end screen and the handoff points forward with no
pickup needed. Write the spoken line so it survives that swap — describe what
the viewer needs next, not the title of a specific video.

The exception is when the next video in the chain is already scripted and
being filmed in the same session. Then name it directly; it'll exist by the
time anyone watches.

**Plan in chains, not singles.** Map a three or four video problem-chain
before making any of them, so every video has a known destination even if it
isn't built yet. Script two at a time and film them together — that puts one
video permanently in hand, and costs a single week of delay to get there.

### The offer ask — roughly one video in four

The old rule put an offer CTA on every video. That's now relaxed: **about one
video in four carries the offer ask, and the rest hand off to the next
video.** Two asks in one ending means neither lands, and a channel that
pitches every time reads as a funnel rather than a resource.

**Standing rule:** one ask per video, verbal, UTM-tagged. No hard sell. No
pressure. No "smash that subscribe button".

**Current destinations (offer stack as of the cohort restructure):**
- The cohort waitlist — the primary destination while places aren't yet open
- The £197 coaching call — for someone who wants to talk now
- A lead magnet, once one exists

The £397 90-Day Account Plan VOD has been retired. Do not reference it,
its price, or its sales page in any script.

**CTA structure:**
- Name what the link is (not just "link in the description")
- One sentence on who it's for and what it does
- That's it

### The lead magnet goes after brick one

Most viewers never reach the last thirty seconds, so an end-of-video mention
of a free download reaches the smallest possible audience.

**The placement is fixed: immediately after brick one closes.** Not in the
hook, because the viewer hasn't been given anything yet and it reads as a
toll gate. Not after brick two or three, because every brick you wait costs
you the viewers who left in the meantime. Brick one's logic half is the
first point where the viewer has had something genuinely useful, and that's
when they're most inclined to believe there's more where it came from.

Pitch it as extra help for them, not as a favour to Mark. Name it, say where
it is, get straight back into the video. The whole interruption is about
seven seconds.

**The lead magnet must be diagnostic, not instructional** — questions to ask
about your own territory, a checklist for spotting the problem. The teaching
itself belongs in the video, given away properly. A download that's really a
withheld chapter of the method contradicts the giveaway rule.

**The lead magnet already exists:** the free PDF on which accounts are
actually costing you sales — `https://mark-young-sales.kit.com/94eaa538e7`.
It's already carried in the video descriptions with UTM tags. What hasn't
been done is moving the *spoken* mention from the end of the video to just
after the first teaching moment. Do that from the next script onward.

**UTM reminder:** Every CTA link must be UTM-tagged. Confirm which
destination applies for this video (waitlist, coaching call, or lead
magnet) and ensure the tracking is in place before the video goes live.

**What the ending must not do:**
- Reference the retired £397 VOD or its sales page
- Carry both an offer ask and a next-video handoff
- Recap what the viewer just watched
- Make claims that contradict the calm, measured tone of the video
- Run for more than 30 seconds

## Depth — The Scar Scale

Since the methodology is given away, the method alone no longer differentiates
anything. Anyone can restate it, and increasingly an AI can. What can't be
restated is thirty years of watching it go right and wrong. Every script
should be checked for depth on four levels.

**S — Surface.** Pure information. "Five ways to grow an account." Correct,
useful, and available anywhere in ten seconds.

**C — Competence.** Showing the process. Walking through the capacity
calculation properly. Better, but any competent sales trainer could do it.

**A — Adversity.** When it breaks, and what happened. "I've watched this go
wrong about thirty times, and it's almost always the same cause." The plan
that didn't survive contact with a real territory. The advice Mark used to
give and stopped giving. This level is currently under-used and it's the
biggest available upgrade to the channel.

**R — Reframe.** Why most people have the problem the wrong way round. This
is already the channel's strongest register — the whole core thesis is a
reframe.

**Treat it as a dial, not a category.** Most videos will need some surface
content to be findable. The instruction is to turn the dial down at least one
click in every script: one real situation, one pattern only years of doing
this would surface, one honest limit of the advice.

**Being honest about the limits buys more trust than another win story.** A
plain "this doesn't work for everyone, and here's who it doesn't work for" is
a sentence no salesman would say, which is exactly why it lands.

## Named Concepts

A script should contain one idea that could only have come from Mark, and it
should have a name.

Naming is not giving anything away — it's the opposite. An unnamed idea gets
absorbed and forgotten; a named one gets attributed and repeated. The stars
people navigate by aren't the brightest ones, they're the ones somebody drew
a shape around and named.

The channel already has candidates half-formed: the busy fool problem, the
false signal, relationship bias. Firm those up, use the same words every
time, and reference them across videos so returning viewers build a
vocabulary and feel like insiders.

A name can take several shapes — a plain two-word label, a rule with a number
in it, a simple diagram, a short list with the count in the title. Pick
whichever the idea actually fits. Don't force one.

**Rule:** the concept must be real. A name on a hollow idea is marketing, and
this audience will smell it.

## Client References

Sprinkle references to real client work throughout the body. Nothing
elaborate — "a rep I worked with last year ran into exactly this", "I've
watched about thirty area managers try that".

Two reasons. First, without them a viewer files Mark as a free teacher —
someone smart and generous whom it would never occur to them to pay. One
casual mention that people pay for this makes hiring him a thought that can
occur. Second, a tip inside a real situation is simply better content than
the tip on its own, and it's the one thing a competitor can't copy.

Aim for at least one per video.

## The Comment Section

Treat replies as public office hours, not admin. Someone asks a real question
under a video and gets a real answer, and every person who reads that
exchange sees Mark's judgement applied to a live situation. That's the thing
the channel is selling, visible for free.

While the channel is small, reply properly to everything — it won't be
possible later. A single considered reply can move someone from "I found this
person" to "I trust this person".

On trolls: they arrive when a video reaches past the core audience, so they're
a growth signal more than an insult. Block, or add the word to the filter list
and never see it again. Don't engage.

## Live Sessions — Deferred

Live teardowns are the strongest format available to a thirty-year
practitioner. Nobody can fake working through a stranger's territory in real
time, and in a feed increasingly full of machine-made content that's the
point.

**Not yet.** Going live to an empty room is dispiriting and there aren't
enough regular viewers to fill one. **Trigger: revisit once videos are
averaging roughly 25+ views.** At that point start with a Q&A — bring ten
questions pulled from the comments to cover the slow opening — and build
towards live territory teardowns, where a viewer's real account numbers get
worked through on air.

Announce a week ahead so it becomes an appointment. The recording lives on the
channel afterwards either way, so a quiet one still produces a video.

## Post-Writing Checklist

Before presenting any draft to Mark:

- [ ] Every sentence is a full sentence — no fragments
- [ ] Contractions are used throughout — check every "cannot", "do not",
      "it is", "have not", "are not", "will not", "did not"
- [ ] Line 1 mirrors the title
- [ ] The hook contains a macro setup that is paid off in the bridge
- [ ] Every brick has a transition (setup), emotion half, and logic half
      (payoff) — or a clear reason why the emotion half was shortened/skipped
- [ ] Every transition stands alone — read cold with the heading covered,
      it still says what's coming and why the viewer should stay
- [ ] No brick payoff is given before the viewer has been set up to want it
- [ ] Every setup in the entire script has a corresponding payoff
- [ ] The method is taught properly — nothing held back to protect the offer
- [ ] The scar-scale dial is turned down at least one click — a real
      situation, a pattern from experience, or an honest limit
- [ ] The script contains one named concept that could only be Mark's
- [ ] At least one client reference appears in the body
- [ ] Worked examples are real territory situations, not Sarah Mitchell
- [ ] No template or worksheet has been handed over as a downloadable asset
- [ ] The bridge lands the emotional conclusion before the CTA
- [ ] There is exactly one ask — either an offer CTA or a next-video
      handoff, never both
- [ ] The video hands off rather than wrapping up, unless this is an
      offer-CTA video (roughly one in four)
- [ ] The lead magnet mention sits immediately after brick one — not later,
      not at the end
- [ ] No borrowed statistics (Mehrabian, seven hours, eleven touchpoints)
- [ ] The word "rep" appears nowhere in the spoken script
- [ ] The UTM-tagged link destination has been confirmed (waitlist,
      £197 call, or lead magnet)
- [ ] The script sounds like Mark when read aloud — not a YouTuber,
      not a trainer, not a copywriter

## Saving to Notion

Once a script section is approved by Mark, save it to Notion immediately.
Do not defer. One section at a time, in standalone artifacts. Create a
child page under the main workflow page using the naming convention:

`🎬 Video [N] — [Title] Script (LOCKED — [Month Year])`

Fetch the page immediately after saving to confirm the content landed.
